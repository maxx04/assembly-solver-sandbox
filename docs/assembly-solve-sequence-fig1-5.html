Every Assembly::AssemblyObject is a document object like any other — FreeCAD's recompute engine calls its execute(), which calls solve(). Fig. 1 walks that one call, top to bottom. Three steps inside it fan out into their own mechanism, each pulled out below: how getJoints() reaches into a nested sub‑assembly (Fig. 2), how getGroundedParts() does the same for grounding (Fig. 3), and how canonicalizeForMbD() reconciles the two different pointers a single real part can have (Fig. 4). Fig. 5 is the reason this diagram exists: what actually happens when two of these solves cover the same physical sub‑tree.

Fig. 1 — main sequence

One solve() call, one document

Triggered by a recompute on a single AssemblyObject. Three steps here each have their own mechanism, cross‑referenced to the right.

Fig. 2 — getJoints(subJoints = true)

Reaching a joint two nesting levels down

A flexible AssemblyLink is not descended into by reading its own (copied) joint list — the recursion steps into the real, linked AssemblyObject instance and asks it. A rigid sub‑assembly is never entered; it counts as one solid part.

Fig. 3 — getGroundedParts()

Grounding didn't reach past one level — until today

A part's Placement.isReadOnly() flag is mirrored down through the copy pipeline, but only reliably one level. A part grounded two levels down used to be structurally invisible to the outer solve. The added call closes exactly that gap.

Fig. 4 — canonicalizeForMbD()

The same part, two pointers — made one

The copy pipeline keeps a local mirror of BoxC inside GrandTop's own document; the joint machinery in Fig. 2 resolves straight to the real BoxC inside Top's document. Both are legitimate ways to arrive at "BoxC" — but the multibody solver needs exactly one pointer per real part, or it opens two disconnected bodies for it.

Fig. 5 — the open problem

Two solves, one real object, no ownership rule

Fig. 1–4 fixed how one solve reaches into nested assemblies. They didn't change how many solves run. Top#Assembly and GrandTop#Assembly are two separate AssemblyObject instances, each with its own mbdAssembly and objectPartMap — and since Fig. 2, both now see the joint living inside Sub. One document recompute calls solve() on both, in sequence, and both are willing to write Sub#BoxB.Placement.

Before the next fix

Fig. 5 is the actual shape of Befund 3. A real fix means deciding who owns a shared sub‑tree's solve — which turns on two things neither of us has pinned down yet:

  • 1 Sequential, or genuinely concurrent? Fig. 5 draws one call stack because that's what the log shows — Top finishes before GrandTop starts, every time. If that's reliably true, this is an ordering problem. If solves can ever overlap across documents, it's a locking problem too, and needs a different fix.
  • 2 Outermost wins, or innermost wins? Either the outermost assembly should be the one true solve and inner levels should just take its result (top‑down), or the level the user is actually editing should solve and hand its result outward (bottom‑up). Fig. 5 shows neither happening reliably today.
